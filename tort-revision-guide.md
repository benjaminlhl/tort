# PART A — INTRODUCTION TO TORT

## 1. What is a tort?

> "Tort is a term applied to a **miscellaneous and more or less unconnected group of civil wrongs, other than breach of contract**, for which a court of law will afford a **remedy in the form of an action for damages**."
> — W Prosser, *Handbook of the Law of Torts* (2nd ed, 1955), p 1

### 1.1 Crime vs tort

| | **Criminal law** | **Tort law** |
|---|---|---|
| Protected interests | **Societal** | **Private** |
| Proceedings initiated by | **The state** | **A private individual** |
| Aim of proceedings | (Typically) **punishment and deterrence** | (Typically) **compensation** (and deterrence) |
| Court order | **Criminal penalty** | **Damages** |
| Standard of proof | **Beyond reasonable doubt** | **Balance of probabilities** |

### 1.2 Contract vs tort

| | **Tort** | **Contract** |
|---|---|---|
| Duty owed to | **The whole world** | **Only the other contracting party** |
| Source of duty | **Imposed by law** | **Agreed by the parties** |

- **Concurrent liability:** liability can arise in **both contract and tort from the same facts**: ***Henderson v Merrett Syndicates Ltd* [1995]**. If so, the **content of the tort duty may be affected by the contract** between P and D.
- The rules on **remoteness, assessment of damages and limitation periods can differ** between contract and tort.
- *Link:* in *Donoghue v Stevenson* (§15), P had **no contract**, so tort was her only route.

## 3. A brief history of tort law

### 3.1 The old system: forms of action (before the 1850s)
In early English common law, **procedure reigned supreme**. To sue, you had to fit your claim into a specific **form of action (writ)**.

| Writ | Covered | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Trespass** | **Direct** harm: immediate, direct interference with a person, land or goods | D throws a log into the road and it **hits you as it falls** |
| **Action on the case** | **Indirect or consequential** harm | D throws a log into the road and leaves it there; you **later trip over it**. **The historical ancestor of modern negligence and nuisance.** |

### 3.2 Legislative reform (19th century)
- **The problem:** if you sued in trespass but the judge decided the injury was actually indirect, **the whole case was thrown out**.
- **Common Law Procedure Act 1852:** began simplifying lawsuits and chipping away at the boundaries between the writs.
- **Judicature Acts 1873–1875:** **fully abolished the forms of action**. Plaintiffs simply **stated the facts** of their claim.

### 3.3 The modern shift (20th century)
- The focus moved **away from *how* the injury happened** (direct or indirect) and **towards D's state of mind**.
  - **Direct interference** became the **intentional torts** (trespass to the person or land, where you intend the physical act).
  - **Indirect injury** evolved into **negligence**: you didn't mean to hurt anyone but **failed to take reasonable care**.
- Tort law became a **system of moral responsibility**. Today the question is "**was it an accident (negligence) or on purpose (intentional tort)?**", not "was it direct or indirect?"

> ***Letang v Cooper* [1965] 1 QB 232 (CA), Lord Denning:** "If he does not inflict injury intentionally, but only unintentionally, the plaintiff has no cause of action today in trespass. His only cause of action is in negligence, and then only on proof of want of reasonable care… it is not enough nowadays for the plaintiff to plead that 'the defendant shot the plaintiff.' He must also allege that he did it **intentionally or negligently**. If intentional, it is the tort of **assault and battery**. If negligent and causing damage, it is the tort of **negligence**."

### 3.4 The dominance of negligence (the last 100 years)
Modern tort law is a **patchwork** of principles and separate torts, **not a single unified theory**. It has been shaped by:
- **Economic and social change:** the Industrial Revolution (heavy machinery, railways) moved tort from neighbour disputes to **industrial accidents**.
- **The rise of the welfare state:** as states provided safety nets (workers' compensation, public healthcare), tort had to adjust **so as not to duplicate state payouts**.
- **The role of insurance:** judges shape tort rules knowing that **insurers, not individuals, will usually pay** (§10).
- **"Compensation culture" concerns:** fear of a litigious society has led courts and legislatures to **restrict liability** to prevent a flood of frivolous claims.

**Negligence now dominates** because it is **highly flexible**. The general **duty of care** adapts to new problems, from defective factory products to medical malpractice.

## 4. Interests protected and classification of torts

### 4.1 Interests protected
- **Physical integrity**
- **Psychological well-being**
- **Freedom of movement**
- **Property**
- **Reputation**
- **Economic interests**

⚠️ **Protection is not absolute.** The interests of **D and the public** must be protected too.

### 4.2 Classification
Torts can be classified by the **protected interest** and by **D's conduct** (intention, negligence or no fault).

| # | Tort | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Trespass** | **Intentional** interference with person or property; the **oldest** tort. (a) **To the person** (personal integrity and freedom): assault, battery, false imprisonment. (b) **To goods** (movable property/chattels). (c) **To land**. |
| 2 | **(Private) nuisance** | **Indirect** interference with the use or enjoyment of land |
| 3 | **Negligence** | Breach of a duty to take reasonable care; **the most pervasive tort** |
| 4 | **Breach of public and statutory duty** | |
| 5 | **Misrepresentation torts** | E.g. deceit, defamation, injurious falsehood |
| 6 | **Intentional damage to economic and other interests** | E.g. interference with contractual relations, conspiracy, intimidation, malicious prosecution |

## 5. Degrees of culpability

Shifting loss through tort **generally relies on fault**. Basing liability on fault is rooted in **individual responsibility and autonomy**.

| Level of fault | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Intentional** | **Purpose or desire** to invade another's protected interest |
| **Reckless** | **Conscious disregard of a high risk of serious harm** |
| **Negligent** | **Unreasonable conduct in light of foreseeable risks** |
| **Strictly liable** | **Liability without fault** |

**Key point from lecture:** of these terms, **negligence is the only one that is itself a tort**. Recklessness and the others are **states of mind or degrees of fault**, not causes of action. In a negligence claim the question is simply: **was D negligent?**

**Where recklessness matters:**
1. **Trespass to the person**
2. **The tort of harassment**
3. **Torts defined by statute**, where the statute sets the required mental state
4. **At the damages stage**, when considering **punitive/exemplary damages** (§12). These may be appropriate where D was **genuinely reckless as to P's wellbeing**, e.g. D ignored safety for the sake of profits.
   - Lecture example: the **McDonald's hot coffee case**. **[CASE TBC]** *(likely* Liebeck v McDonald's Restaurants *(1994, USA))*

## 6. Aims and functions of tort law

**The fundamental question:** why **shift a financial loss** from the injured party to the person who caused it? Tort law **intervenes directly in the real world** to do this.

### 6.1 The three core aims

| Aim | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Compensation** (fairness and justice) | Making P **"whole" again** by shifting the financial burden of the harm to the wrongdoer |
| **Deterrence and prevention** | **Discouraging risky behaviour** and preventing future accidents **by making negligence costly** |
| **Vindication of rights** | **Defining, protecting and acknowledging private rights** by redressing infringements through damages. It sends a message that rights matter. |

### 6.2 Glanville Williams (Welsh legal scholar, 1951): four purposes of a tort action

| Purpose | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Appeasement** | A legal avenue for redress that **avoids private vengeance** or physical retaliation |
| **Justice** | **Moral accountability and fairness** |
| **Deterrence** | **Warning others** against similar conduct |
| **Compensation** | **Restoring the victim's position** |

> Tort law is "a **complex set of ethical principles of personal responsibility (and freedom)** which concern how people may, ought, or ought not to behave in their dealings with others." — **Peter Cane**

### 6.3 Compensation
- The **overarching objective** is compensating P for loss or injury **wrongfully caused by D**.
- **Only legally recognised injuries or losses are compensable** (§9).
- ***Livingstone v Rawyards Coal Co* (1880) 5 App Cas 25, Lord Blackburn:** damages are "the sum of money which will **put the party who has been injured… in the same position as he would have been in if he had not sustained the wrong**".

### 6.4 Doing justice
- ***Vellino v Chief Constable of Greater Manchester* [2002] 1 WLR 218 (CA), Sedley LJ (dissenting):** the police negligently allowed an arrested man to try to escape, and he was injured. The majority denied recovery because of his own offending. Sedley LJ said: "To deny the claimant redress in such a situation because of his own offending is both to **make him an outlaw and to reward the misconduct of his captors**. To **apportion responsibility**… is in my view **to do justice**."
- *Link:* this is related to **illegality** as a DOC filter (§14).

### 6.5 Deterrence
- **Specific deterrence:** aimed at the **individual D**.
- **General deterrence:** aimed at the **wider public**.
- **How it works for businesses:** liability **raises the cost of negligence**. Businesses factor in the cost of lawsuits, costs are often **passed on to consumers** (e.g. food prices rise by X%), and if the cost is high enough the risky behaviour stops.
- **Limits:**
  - The deterrent value is **debatable**, **particularly where liability insurance is mandatory** (§10).
  - **Market deterrence** has **some value**, e.g. in **product liability**.
  - Deterrence may be **strengthened by exemplary or punitive damages** on top of compensation.
  - **Every repeated tort shows that deterrence has failed** in that case. Why? D is **insured**, or **payouts are too low**. It may work, but it is hard to prove.

## 7. Economic perspectives

Tort also operates on a **macro level**, balancing **societal costs and behaviours**.

### 7.1 Loss shifting vs loss distribution

| | **Loss shifting** | **Loss distribution (spreading)** |
|---|---|---|
| Idea | Move the loss **from P to the person who ought to bear it, or who can better bear it** | Spread the loss **across a larger group** so it is **easier to bear** |
| How | A standard tort claim, P v D | **Insurance** or **the market (corporate pricing)**. **"Tort tax"**: a coffee chain raises prices to fund lawsuits, so all coffee drinkers bear the loss. **New Zealand**: a **government agency pays compensation** instead of P suing D. **Some HK Ordinances** do this directly. **[CHECK which]** |
| Justice theory | **Corrective justice** (reversing wrongs; D repairs the loss D caused) | **Distributive justice** (socially just allocation of resources). **But some scholars argue distributive justice is alien to tort law.** |

### 7.2 Administrative / legal efficiency
- Does the system **resolve disputes effectively without excessive cost or delay**?
- Would **other methods** (e.g. a no-fault scheme) do better? Tort **could be reformed**.

### 7.3 Economic efficiency
- Tort should create **market incentives for optimal safety** **without entirely stifling useful, productive activities**.

### 7.4 Law and economics
- An **instrumentalist** account (tort exists to achieve a social good) **originating in the US (Posner)**.
- Tort law should support the **efficient allocation of resources**.
- It **examines and critiques doctrine through this lens**, e.g. **preventing accidents through the "cheapest cost avoider"**.
- **Assumption:** people are **rational actors** who respond to **financial incentives**.
- **Tort does not aim to eliminate all harm.** It **balances costs and benefits**.
  - **Car example:** capping cars at 10 km/h would eliminate about 99% of accidents but **destroy the car industry**. Neither car owners nor society want that.
- **Optimal accident prevention:** take precautions **up to the point where the cost of an additional precaution exceeds the expected harm it prevents**. Tort law **expects accidents to happen**. The key question for a company: is it cheaper to **prevent** or to **compensate**?
- *Extra:* often expressed as the **Learned Hand formula (B < P × L)**. See also *Bolton v Stone* (§24.4).

## 8. Theoretical perspectives

### 8.1 Rights-based theory (Stevens, Beever)
- A **more recent** account.
- **Tort law exists to protect and vindicate rights**: bodily integrity, property, reputation, autonomy, etc.
- A **breach of a duty owed to others** gives rise to a **remedy** (usually damages).
- **A claim exists even without damage**, because the wrong is the **infringement of P's right**.
  - **Trespass to land:** crossing onto a neighbour's land, even by accident.
  - **Battery:** a punch or any unwanted touching, even with no real injury.
- **Compatible with corrective justice**, since both rest on **moral and ethical** considerations. Rights theory focuses more on **explaining the content (purpose) of the rules**.

### 8.2 Utilitarianism (the alternative to rights-based theory)
- **Does not believe in rights.** Only **pleasure and pain** exist, so the aim is to maximise pleasure and minimise pain.
- Focuses on **maximising overall welfare or utility**, which leads to **distributive justice** and **economic efficiency**.
- Utilitarians still **support a tort system** because it maximises welfare.

### 8.3 Corrective justice (Weinrib)
- A **highly influential** account.
- Tort law **rectifies wrongs** suffered by the victim at the hands of the wrongdoer. It restores P to their former position and **restores moral equality** between the parties.
- **Moral basis:** fairness, and **redressing the imbalance created by the wrong**. It focuses on the **interpersonal relationship**: justice **between two individuals**.
- **Weakness:** it explains many aspects of tort but **does not tell us what counts as a wrong, or why**.

### 8.4 Distributive justice
- **Fair allocation of resources, risks and burdens** across **society**.

| | **Corrective justice** | **Distributive justice** |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | **Between two individuals** | **Across society** |
| Mechanism | **Loss shifting** | **Loss distribution** |

### 8.5 Fault-based liability
- Shifting loss **generally depends on fault**:
  - **Intention**, which corresponds to an action in **trespass**
  - **Negligence**, which corresponds to an action in **negligence**
- The philosophical basis is **individual responsibility and autonomy**.
- **Strict liability** = liability **regardless of fault**. It is used for activities so risky that P should recover even if D took reasonable care.

## 9. Limits of compensation

**Only *legally recognised* losses are compensated.**

- **Psychiatric harm** and **pure economic loss** are **not recoverable in many situations**, for **policy reasons**, above all the **floodgates** concern (§18.5).
- **Pure economic loss** is a simple **loss of money**. It is generally recoverable **only where P relied on advice from someone who assumed responsibility** (*Hedley Byrne*, §16.1).
- **Mere "injured feelings" or simple grief:** e.g. a sibling is injured and you feel sad or angry **without a recognised psychiatric illness**. This is **excluded entirely** (§14).

## 10. The impact of insurance

### 10.1 Types

| Type | What it does | Subrogation |
|---|---|---|
| **First-party (indemnity) insurance** | **Indemnifies the insured for their own loss, without reference to the tort system**. E.g. your property is damaged and you claim on your policy. | The insurer can **pursue the insured's rights against the tortfeasor**, stepping into the victim's shoes. **Insurers may be the real parties suing.** |
| **Third-party (liability) insurance** | **Indemnifies the insured against sums they become liable to pay as damages to third persons**. E.g. you crash into someone and your insurer pays. **Often compulsory** (e.g. motor insurance). **[CHECK: HK example, e.g. Motor Vehicles Insurance (Third Party Risks) Ordinance (Cap 272)]** | Enables the insurer to **claim contribution or indemnity from other persons involved**. |

### 10.2 Effects
- Insurance **undermines a system of liability based on fault** (individual responsibility) **but promotes the compensation aim**.
  - Essay point: if D is insured and the insurer pays, **where is the corrective justice?**
- **Courts do not follow a consistent policy** on how far insurance should be taken into account.
  - In practice, judges tend to be **more willing to find a DOC where insurance is mandatory**, even while saying it doesn't matter. See *Nettleship v Weston* (§25.2).

## 11. Parties: who can sue and be sued; immunities

### 11.1 Who can sue?
**Any person with legal capacity** whose legal interests have been **wrongfully interfered with**, including **individuals** and **legal persons** (corporations, incorporated bodies).

| Who | Authority |
|---|---|
| **Newborns**, for harm suffered **in utero** | **LARCO (Cap 23), Part IVA** |
| **The estate** of a deceased person, for harm suffered **before death** | **LARCO s 20** |
| **Spouses** suing each other. E.g. a husband drives negligently and his wife, a passenger, is injured; she can sue him. | **Married Persons Status Ordinance (Cap 182)** |

### 11.2 Who can be sued?
**Any wrongdoer with legal capacity:**

| Defendant | HK / leading authority |
|---|---|
| **Individuals, including children** (no absolute immunity) | *McHale v Watson* [1966] ALR 513; *Leung Kwok Lung v Ling Wai* [2010] HKEC 544 |
| **Corporations** | *Chau Chui Ping v Cathay Pacific Airways Ltd* (2006) HCPI 261/2003 |
| **Incorporated bodies** (e.g. building owners' corporations; something falls from a building onto you) | *Aberdeen Winner Investments Co Ltd v Incorporated Owners of Albert House* [2004] 3 HKLRD 910 |
| **The Government** (e.g. you slip and fall while visiting LegCo) | *Wong Tai Wai v HKSAR Government* (2004) CACV 19 & 247/2003 |
| **Government officials** | *Li Ngan Shui Brumen v Official Receiver* [1995] 1 HKC 133 |
| **Statutory bodies** | *Cheng Wai Leung v Pneumoconiosis Compensation Fund Board* [1990] 2 HKC 333 |

### 11.3 Immunities
- An **immunity** protects a person **from being sued**. Because it **leaves an injured party without redress**, it **must be based on compelling public policy grounds**. Immunity is **usually not available**.

**Statutory immunities**

| Immunity | Source |
|---|---|
| **Parental immunity:** a **mother** who negligently causes **pre-birth injury** to her child is immune from suit by the child | **LARCO s 22B(1)** |
| **Legislators' immunity:** "Members of the Legislative Council… shall be immune from legal action **in respect of their statements at meetings of the Council**." It covers **only statements**, to promote free debate. | **Basic Law, Article 77** |
| **Foreign state (sovereign) immunity** | |
| **Limited immunities for the HKSAR Government**, e.g. exercising **judicial functions** (the former "Crown" immunity). The Government **may generally be sued in tort**. | |
| **Trade unions**, in relation to **trade disputes** | |

**Common law "immunities" (no DOC)**
- In negligence, a DOC has been **denied for certain defendants in certain contexts**. This has **the same practical effect as an immunity**:
  - **Police**, for conduct in **investigating or preventing crime**: ***Hill v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire* [1989] AC 53 (HL)**; ***Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire Police* [2018] AC 736** (§19).
  - **Soldiers engaging the enemy** in hostilities: ***Smith v Ministry of Defence* [2014] 1 AC 52**.
- **Common law immunities can be revoked or restricted.** E.g. **advocates' immunity** has been **abolished in the UK** but **not yet decided in HK**: ***HKSAR v Hung Chan Wa* (2006) 9 HKCFAR 614** (§18.4).

## 12. Remedies

### 12.1 Damages: the standard remedy
- **Damages** = a **monetary sum** awarded to P to **compensate for the violation of a right**.
- **Damage** = the **actual harm or loss** P suffered because of the infringement.

| Type | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Compensatory damages** | Put P in the position they would have been in (*Livingstone*, §6.3). Divided into: **special damages**, which are **quantifiable** losses already incurred (e.g. receipts); and **general damages**, which are **non-quantifiable and future** losses. |
| **Aggravated damages** | **A type of compensation** for **aggravated harm**, e.g. extra distress caused by D's conduct |
| **Nominal damages** | **No real damage** (e.g. trespass). A **very small sum** (e.g. HK$100) marks the infringement of the right. |
| **Restitutionary damages** | Strip D of an **unjust gain** |
| **Exemplary (punitive) damages** | **Not compensation.** They **punish D** and **make a statement**. Linked to **recklessness** (§5) and **deterrence** (§6.5). |

> **Aggravated vs exemplary:** aggravated damages **compensate P**; exemplary damages **punish D**.

### 12.2 Injunctions and declarations
- **Non-monetary, forward-looking** remedies. They aim to **prevent imminent wrongs**, **stop ongoing torts**, or **establish legal rights** before significant damage occurs.
- Used where **damages would not give adequate relief**, e.g. a **nuisance** claim seeking an **injunction**.
- May require **judicial monitoring**.
- **Damages look back** (compensation for past harm). **Injunctions and declarations look forward.**

# PART B — NEGLIGENCE: DUTY OF CARE

## 13. Negligence: overview and exam checklist

- **The dominant modern tort.**
- Imposes liability on D for **carelessly causing harm** (loss or injury) to P.
- The focus on **fault** (D's conduct) means **falling short of an objective standard of care**.

### 13.1 The elements

> ***Luen Hing Fat Coating & Finishing Factory v Waan Chuen Ming* (2011) 14 HKCFAR 14, Bokhary PJ (HK CFA):** "Broadly speaking, the essential elements of a successful claim in the tort of negligence are **(i) a duty of care** owed by the defendant to the plaintiff, **(ii) breach** of that duty, **(iii) damage** suffered by the plaintiff as a result and **(iv) such damage not being too remote**."

- **If P proves duty, breach, factual causation and no remoteness, D is liable unless D establishes a defence.** If no defence applies, **damages** are assessed.

### 13.2 ✅ The exam checklist: the conventional approach
***Khan v Meadows* [2021] UKSC 21, [79] (Lord Burrows)** *(also reported as* Meadows v Khan*)*

| # | Question | Label |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Was there a **duty of care** owed by D to P? | Duty of care |
| 2 | Was there a **breach** of the duty of care? | Breach / standard of care |
| 3 | Was the damage **factually caused** by the breach? | Factual causation |
| 4 | Was the damage **too remote** from the breach? | Remoteness |
| 5 | Was the damage **legally caused** by the breach? | Legal causation / intervening cause |
| 6 | Was the damage **within the scope of the duty of care**? | Scope of duty (§21) |
| 7 | Are there **any defences**? | Defences |

> ⚠️ **Marks are awarded for each component, so always go through every step.** **Always state whether any defences apply, even if none do** ("No defences appear to apply on these facts").

- There are **two types of causation**: **factual** and **legal**.
- **Scope of duty** is placed at **Question 6** by Lord Burrows, but it is closely **related to Question 1**. **Placement is not universally agreed** (§21.5).

## 14. Duty of care as a legal filter

The DOC **acts as a legal filter**. It lets courts **set boundaries on negligence liability** by restricting or excluding claims in **three categories**:

| Category | What is filtered | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Type of conduct** | **Acts vs omissions:** courts are **generally reluctant to impose liability for a mere failure to act** (§20). **Illegality:** liability may be **completely excluded** if the parties were in a **joint illegal enterprise**. | You walk past a **drowning baby**: at common law there is **no DOC to rescue it**, so the parents cannot sue. **Two drug dealers** packing drugs, one accidentally stabs the other: **no DOC** because of the illegal enterprise. **[CASE TBC]** (See also *Vellino*, §6.4.) |
| **2. Type of loss** | **Excluded entirely:** non-actionable harms like **mere "injured feelings"**. **Strictly restricted:** harder-to-prove or expansive losses, i.e. **pure economic loss** and **psychiatric injury**. | Psychiatric harm from seeing a close relative severely injured: often **no DOC owed to you** (*Alcock v CC South Yorkshire* [1992] 1 AC 310, §18.3). |
| **3. Type of person / relationship** | **Excluding or limiting D's responsibility** towards certain groups, such as **those voluntarily participating in inherently risky activities** | E.g. *Grimes v Hawkins* (§18.4), an adult diving into a pool |

## 15. *Donoghue v Stevenson* (1932) and the neighbour principle

### 15.1 Before 1932
- A DOC was owed **only if the P–D relationship fell into a recognised category**.
  - E.g. ***Heaven v Pender* (1883) 11 QBD 503 (CA)**: the majority found liability because P was D's **"invitee"**. Contrast **Brett MR's "larger principle"**, a forerunner of the neighbour principle.
- In 1932 Lord Atkin formulated the **neighbour principle**, a **general conception of relationships giving rise to a DOC**. This **opened the gates** and led to a **dramatic expansion of negligence**.

### 15.2 Facts
- In **August 1928**, Mrs May Donoghue went to a café in **Paisley, Scotland**, where **a friend bought her** a ginger beer ice cream float.
- The ginger beer came in an **opaque dark glass bottle**. After she drank most of it, the rest was poured out and **the decomposed remains of a snail** slid out.
- She suffered **severe shock and gastroenteritis**.
- **Legal issue:** she had **no contract** with the café owner or the manufacturer, because **her friend bought the drink**, so she **could not sue in contract**. She sued the **manufacturer, Mr David Stevenson**, claiming he failed in his **duty to keep his products safe for ultimate consumers**.

### 15.3 The neighbour principle (**learn verbatim**)

> ***Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932] AC 562, Lord Atkin:** "You must take **reasonable care** to avoid **acts or omissions** which you can **reasonably foresee** would be likely to injure your **neighbour**. Who, then, in law is my neighbour? The answer seems to be – **persons who are so closely and directly affected by my act that I ought reasonably to have them in contemplation as being so affected** when I am directing my mind to the acts or omissions which are called in question."

- **Reasonableness is the essence of negligence.** If you take **reasonable care**, you avoid liability.
- **In modern terms:** your neighbour is **anyone you should have in mind** when deciding whether to act, **because you would expect them to be closely and directly affected**.
  - *Example:* deciding whether to drive especially fast to the hospital, consider **everyone closely or directly affected by your driving**.
- **Who your neighbour is changes for every act or omission.**

### 15.4 The narrow ratio

> **Lord Atkin:** "A **manufacturer of products** which he sells in such a form as to show that he intends them to **reach the ultimate consumer in the form in which they left him** with **no reasonable possibility of intermediate examination**, and with the knowledge that the absence of reasonable care in the preparation or putting up of the products will result in an injury to the consumer's life or property, **owes a duty to the consumer to take reasonable care**."

| | **Broad ratio** | **Narrow ratio** |
|---|---|---|
| Content | The **neighbour principle**: a general test for a DOC | **Manufacturer → ultimate consumer** |
| Significance | Foundation of modern negligence | **Foundation of the modern commercial economy.** Consumers can **trust that products meet a standard of reasonable care**, from food and drink (Coca-Cola, crisps) to non-food items (earbuds bought online). |

## 16. Landmark decisions after 1932

After *Donoghue*, a duty **based on reasonable foreseeability of harm and proximity** was accepted and **gradually expanded**. Courts then had to work out **its limits**, because of the **floodgates** concern.

### 16.1 *Hedley Byrne & Co v Heller & Partners* [1964] AC 465: pure economic loss
- Negligence liability for a **misstatement causing pure economic loss** where there is a **special relationship**.
- PEL is **generally not recoverable**, **unless** there is a special relationship with an adviser who **assumed responsibility**, and the adviser was negligent (e.g. did no proper research).

### 16.2 *Home Office v Dorset Yacht Co Ltd* [1970] AC 1004: omissions and third-party acts
**Facts**
- **Ten Borstal trainees** (young offenders with escape histories) were working on **Brownsea Island** under the supervision of **three Home Office officers**.
- **In direct breach of instructions, all three officers went to sleep**, leaving the trainees unsupervised.
- **Seven escaped**, boarded a moored yacht, and **crashed it into P's yacht, the "Silver Mist"**. The owners sued the Home Office, which (unlike the trainees) had the resources to pay.
- **Preliminary issue:** was a DOC owed?

**Held (Lord Diplock): DOC owed**
- There was a **special relationship between the officers and the boys**, giving the Home Office **direct authority and physical control** over them.
- There was an **immediate and manifest risk of damage to yacht owners nearby** if the officers neglected their control. The Home Office should have **foreseen the impact on its "neighbours"**.
- **Public policy did not require blanket immunity.** **Broad administrative policy decisions are shielded**, but **clear disregard of operational instructions is actionable**.

**Takeaways**
1. **An exception to the omissions rule.** There is generally no liability for **failing to prevent third parties causing harm**, but a **special relationship of control** creates an exception.
2. **Causation and third-party acts.** A **deliberate criminal act by a third party does not break the chain of causation** (*novus actus interveniens*) if it was the **highly probable consequence of D's negligence**. The boys' acts being criminal **did not get D off the hook**. *(Relevant again in Week 9.)*
3. **The operational vs policy distinction:**

| **Policy (allocation of resources)** | **Operational (negligent execution of duties)** |
|---|---|
| **Non-justiciable.** You **cannot sue the Government over a policy decision you disagree with**. | **Reviewable.** A claim **can proceed**. |
| *E.g.* you trip in a HK park and argue "if the Government hadn't spent so much helping the wealthy, I wouldn't have tripped". **This fails.** | *E.g.* a **particular failure of maintenance** caused the trip. **This may proceed.** |

### 16.3 *Anns v Merton LBC* [1978] AC 728: the two-stage test
1. Is there a **sufficient relationship of proximity**? If so, there is a **prima facie duty**.
2. Are there **public policy considerations that negate or restrict** the duty?

- This test **proved too wide** and was **replaced by *Caparo***.

### 16.4 *Caparo Industries plc v Dickman* [1990] 2 AC 605: the three-stage approach
1. **Foreseeability** of harm to P
2. A relationship of **proximity** between P and D
3. It is **"fair, just and reasonable"** to impose a DOC. **These words call for public policy considerations**, looking **beyond the two parties** to society as a whole.

> **Lord Bridge (at 618):** "[I]n addition to the **foreseeability of damage**, necessary ingredients in any situation giving rise to a duty of care are that there should exist between the party owing the duty and the party to whom it is owed a relationship characterised by the law as one of '**proximity**' or '**neighbourhood**' and that the situation should be one in which the court considers it **fair, just and reasonable** that the law should impose a duty **of a given scope** on the one party for the benefit of the other."

### 16.5 *Caparo* in Hong Kong

**HK follows *Caparo*:** ***Luen Hing Fat Coating & Finishing Factory Ltd v Waan Chuen Ming* (2011) 14 HKCFAR 14**

> **Bokhary PJ:** "No common law landmark, not even one as respected… as Lord Atkin's 'neighbour' statement, can be expected to **stand alone and sufficient on its own forever**… the courts will, as they must, **develop the common law to provide such fresh or adapted solutions as may be needed to cope with new problems** as and when they emerge. Subject of course to that, **I would adopt the *Caparo* approach**…"

> **Bokhary PJ (holistic view):** "Ultimately it is necessary to **stand back and take a holistic view** of foreseeability, proximity and the need to be satisfied that it would be fair, just and reasonable to impose a duty of care." The three matters "**overlap with each other and are really facets of the same thing**" (Saville LJ, endorsed by Lord Steyn in *Marc Rich & Co v Bishop Rock Marine* [1996] AC 211). Proximity is "**an umbrella**, covering a number of disparate circumstances in which the relationship between the parties is so close that it is just and reasonable to permit recovery" (McLachlin J, *Canadian National Railway v Norsk Pacific Steamship* [1992] 1 SCR 1021).

**Summarised in *Yeung Kai Yuen v Cinerent Ltd* [2014] HKEC 633 (Andrew Li J), [63]–[68]:**
- *Caparo* laid down the **"fairness and three stage test"**.
- Justice and reasonableness is a test of "**ordinary reason and common sense**" (Saville J, *Minories Finance v Arthur Young* [1989] 2 All ER 105). The court **weighs all relevant factors and exercises judicial judgment**.
- *Caparo* has been applied in *White v Jones*, *McFarlane v Tayside Health Board*, *Mitchell v Glasgow City Council* and others.
- **The CFA in *Luen Hing Fat* held that FJR is an intrinsic element of the DOC and can be used to *ground* a duty**, whether **no duty previously existed** or **a duty had previously been denied**.

**Critique:** Po Jen Yap, "Negligence in Hong Kong: Turning *Caparo* on its Head?" (2011) 127 LQR 358
- ***Caparo* intended** the law to develop "**incrementally and by analogy with established categories**, rather than by a massive extension of a prima facie duty of care restrained only by indefinable 'considerations'".
- **Bokhary PJ "turned *Caparo* on its head"** by using **policy arguments to *expand* liability** and "provide such fresh or adapted solutions as may be needed".
- 💡 **Essay point:** HK's approach to FJR may be **more expansive** than the UK's post-*Robinson* approach.

## 17. The current approach to establishing a DOC

### 17.1 *Robinson* and *N v Poole*: *Caparo* is not a universal test
If the existence (or non-existence) of a DOC is **well established by precedent, follow the precedent**. In **novel or contentious** situations, courts decide on expansion **by analogy with decided cases**, using the *Caparo* approach.

> ***Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire Police* [2018] UKSC 4, [2018] AC 736, [100] (Lord Hughes):** "…it is **neither necessary nor appropriate** to treat *Caparo* as requiring the application of its familiar three-stage examination **afresh to every action brought**. **Where the law is clear** that a particular relationship, or recurrent factual situation, gives rise to a duty of care, **there is no occasion to resort to *Caparo***, at least unless the court is being invited to depart from previous authority."

> ***Robinson*, [26] (Lord Reed):** "…there are many situations in which it has been clearly established that a duty of care is or is not owed: for example, **by motorists to other road users, by manufacturers to consumers, by employers to their employees, and by doctors to their patients**… It is therefore **unnecessary and inappropriate to reconsider whether the existence of the duty is fair, just and reasonable**…"

> ***Robinson*, [21] (Lord Reed):** "The proposition that there is a *Caparo* test which applies to all claims in the modern law of negligence… **is mistaken**… that understanding of the case **mistakes the whole point of *Caparo***, which was to **repudiate the idea that there is a single test**… and instead to adopt an approach based… on **precedent**, and on the development of the law **incrementally and by analogy with established authorities**."

> ***N v Poole Borough Council* [2020] AC 780, [64] (Lord Reed):** "*Robinson* did not lay down any new principle of law… *Caparo* did not impose a universal tripartite test… but recommended an **incremental approach to novel situations**, based on the use of **established categories of liability as guides, by analogy**, to the existence and scope of a duty of care… The question whether the imposition of a duty of care would be fair, just and reasonable **forms part of the assessment of whether such an incremental step ought to be taken**… in the ordinary run of cases, courts should **apply established principles of law, rather than basing their decisions on their assessment of the requirements of public policy**."

### 17.2 Step-by-step method

```
STEP 1  Does the situation fall within an ESTABLISHED CATEGORY (DOC or no-DOC)?
          ├── YES → follow the precedent. No need for Caparo (Robinson [100]).
          └── NO / BORDERLINE / SCOPE UNCLEAR → go to Step 2
STEP 2  INCREMENTAL APPROACH: reason by analogy from the closest established categories
STEP 3  Use the Caparo factors to guide and structure the analysis
          (foreseeability → proximity → fair, just and reasonable)
```

**Established categories (*Robinson* [26]):**
- **Motorists → other road users.** In a road-accident problem, start with DOC by citing precedent, **not *Caparo***.
- **Manufacturers → consumers** (*Donoghue*)
- **Employers → employees**
- **Doctors → patients**

### 17.3 The incremental approach in HK: *Liu Shih Teng v HKCC Dotcod Ltd* [2022] HKCFI 474
- **Issue:** can a **restaurant operator** be liable in negligence for **serving alcohol to an intoxicated patron** who then **fell down a staircase and suffered fatal injuries** ("alcohol liability")?
- The court held this was **not a situation where precedent clearly controlled**. International common law authority is **divergent** on whether alcohol-serving outlets or commercial hosts owe a general DOC to patrons.
- **Ng J:** "In my view, **the local courts will have to find its own way and set its own incremental pace** for the development of the law in this area."
- **Lesson:** survey the relevant precedents. If the area is **not clearly settled**, the court **cannot simply rely on established precedent**. It moves forward **incrementally**, looking at the issue afresh.

### 17.4 Worked example: is it an established category? *Darnley v Croydon Health Services NHS Trust* [2018] UKSC 50, [2019] AC 831
> **Facts:** a hospital receptionist **carelessly gave a wrong (too long) estimate of the waiting time**. A person in medical need **left** and **suffered injury**. Does this fall within the established duty of a **healthcare provider not to cause physical injury to a patient**?

1. **Established category?** Doctor → patient is **established**. Receptionist → patient is **not a common category**.
2. **But** the law imposes a DOC on the **healthcare provider towards the patient**, **without distinguishing between medical and non-medical staff**:

> "…as soon as the appellant had attended at the respondent's A & E department seeking medical attention… had provided the information requested by the receptionist and had been **'booked in'**, he was **accepted into the system** and entered into a relationship with the respondent of **patient and health care provider**. The damage complained of is **physical injury and not economic loss**. This is a **distinct and recognisable situation in which the law imposes a duty of care**… it is **not appropriate to distinguish… between medical and non-medical staff**."

3. **Scope of duty:** the duty **extends to not giving misleading information** that may foreseeably cause physical injury (§21.2).

> 💡 **Exam technique:** where the category is borderline, **argue it both ways**. First argue it falls within an established category, using *Darnley*-style reasoning (closeness of the relationship; whether P was "booked into the system"). Then say: "Alternatively, if this is a novel situation, the court would proceed incrementally, guided by *Caparo*…" and work through the three factors.

## 18. The *Caparo* factors in detail

### 18.1 Not a precise test: "convenient labels"
> ***Caparo*, Lord Bridge:** "[T]he concepts of proximity and fairness… are **not susceptible of any such precise definition** as would be necessary to give them utility as practical tests but amount in effect to little more than **convenient labels to attach to the features of different specific situations** which, on a detailed consideration of all the circumstances, the law recognises **pragmatically** as giving rise to a duty of care **of a given scope**."

The approach is therefore **holistic** (*Luen Hing Fat*, §16.5).

### 18.2 Foreseeability
- **Objective test:** would a **reasonable person in D's position** have foreseen harm to P?
- **P must prove** that **she was a foreseeable victim** and that **harm to her was a foreseeable consequence** of D's negligence.
- **Neither the exact harm nor the exact way it came about needs to be foreseeable.** It is enough that **some harm was possible and to be anticipated**.

> ***Airport Authority v Western Air Ltd* [2020] UKPC 29, [43] (Lord Kerr):** "Although the **theft of an aircraft is a highly unusual occurrence**, the **exact nature of harm need not be precisely foreseen**. It is enough that **the possibility of harm to unguarded aircraft was to be anticipated**. The very existence of a **perimeter fence, restricted access to the airfield and the provision of patrols** are all testament to the ready foreseeability of the occurrence."

- **In short:** D's negligence allowed an aircraft to be **stolen**. D argued the event was too unusual to be foreseeable, and the argument was **rejected**.

### 18.3 Proximity
- Proximity is **elusive and difficult to define**, yet **often the critical factor**. A court may find **foreseeability but no DOC because proximity is lacking**.
- It focuses on the **nature of the relationship** between P and D, and can arise from:

| Source of proximity | Example |
|---|---|
| **Closeness in time or space** (physical) | |
| **Closeness of personal relationship** | |
| **Circumstantial closeness** | |
| **Causal closeness** | |
| **Representations or assumption of responsibility** | The police "we'll watch your house" example (below); *Hedley Byrne* |
| **P's reasonable reliance or vulnerability** | *Western Air*; *Lam Pak Keung* |

**Proximity is fact-sensitive: *Airport Authority v Western Air* [2020] UKPC 29, [42] (Lord Kerr)**
> "The proximity of relationship… is readily established: the [Airport Authority] was **responsible for security at the airport**; it was **aware that [Western Air's] plane would be parked airside**… aware that **Western Air could not (and did not) provide private security**… it ought to have been conscious that it was **the sole agency into whose care the safety of the aeroplane fell**. The respondent was therefore **uniquely dependent** on the appellant to ensure the safeguarding of its property."

**Assumption of responsibility**
> ***Michael v Chief Constable of South Wales* [2015] UKSC 2, [164] (Lord Kerr):** "It has been recognised that **proximity of relationship can exist where there is a voluntary assumption of responsibility by the police** but in cases where this issue has arisen, **rules have been strictly applied to restrict its ambit**."

- **Police patrol example (lecture):**
  - A neighbourhood suffers burglaries and police patrol **occasionally**. Your house is burgled and you sue. **This fails: no proximity**, because there is **no proximity between the police and every member of society**.
  - **But** if officers on duty **volunteer** ("Don't worry, we'll watch your house") and the house is then burgled, your case is **stronger**, because the police **created proximity** by that promise.
  - Even then, the rules are **"strictly applied"** (*Michael*). **[CHECK: the notes end "but fails". Confirm whether the lecturer said this claim would ultimately still fail.]**

**Vulnerability of P**
> ***Lam Pak Keung v Ip Tsz Ping* [2016] 3 HKLRD 139 (Cheung JA), citing *Luen Hing Fat*:** "…the fact of **personal safety being at stake is always significant**… the policy considerations in favour of personal safety are naturally very powerful. This is particularly so where the person is in a **vulnerable position**, has **no reasonable means of protecting himself**, and thus is at a **disadvantage**."

**Special cases: close proximity required**

| Type of case | Authority |
|---|---|
| **Omissions** (failure to act) | ***Hill v CC West Yorkshire* [1989] AC 53** |
| **Pure economic loss** | ***Hedley Byrne v Heller* [1964] AC 465** |
| **Psychiatric injury** | ***Alcock v CC South Yorkshire* [1992] 1 AC 310** |

> **Important point:** *Caparo* is **not only a limiting tool**. **Proximity (and, in HK, FJR) can be used to *create or expand* a DOC**. This is **incremental expansion**.

### 18.4 Fair, just and reasonable (public policy)
**The idea**
- The third question looks at **public policy beyond the parties**: the **broader social implications** of recognising or refusing a DOC.
- **Why look beyond the parties?** Because of **precedent**. Once a court **recognises a new DOC**, **it applies to everyone**, and later courts won't need *Caparo*.
- It is inevitably somewhat **subjective**, because it depends on the judge's view of what public policy requires.

> ***Barrett v Enfield LBC* [2001] 2 AC 550, 559 (Lord Browne-Wilkinson):** whether it is FJR "depends on **weighing in the balance the total detriment to the public interest in all cases from holding such class liable** in negligence **as against the total loss to all would-be plaintiffs** if they are not to have a cause of action…"

**How judges actually decide**
> ***Michael v CC South Wales* [2015] AC 1732, [160] (Lord Kerr):** "…courts are called on to make judgments that are informed by what they consider to be **preponderant policy considerations**. Some assessment has to be made of **what a judge considers the public interest to be; what detriment would be caused to that interest if liability were held to exist; and what harm would be done to claimants if they are denied a remedy**… These calculations are **not conducted according to fixed principle**. They will frequently… be made **without empirical evidence**. For the most part, they will be **instinctual reactions** to any given set of circumstances."

- **Criticism:** courts **lack the staff to research and produce statistical analysis**, so policymaking is instinctive.

**Broader social implications: *Kylie Grimes v David Hawkins* [2011] EWHC 2004 (QB), [97] (Thirlwall J)**
- **Facts:** P **dived too shallowly** into D's **pool** and was seriously injured. She sued for failing to **lock the pool** and failing to put up **"no diving" signs**.
- **Foreseeable? Yes.** **Proximity? Arguably yes** (it was D's home). **FJR? No:**

> "…the claimant was **an adult**. She did something which carried an **obvious risk**. She **chose, voluntarily, to dive** when, how and where she did, knowing the risks involved… it would **not be fair, just or reasonable** to impose upon the defendant a duty of care… which required him to **put his pool out of bounds at night, or to prohibit adults from diving** into the pool."

- **Consider the consequences for other pool owners** in society.

**Personal injury: FJR is rarely an obstacle**
- Claims for **acts directly causing foreseeable physical injury** do **not generally raise policy concerns**. FJR **plays a bigger role where the harm is *not* foreseeable physical injury** (economic loss, psychiatric harm).
- **Why?** Physical injury is **harder to exaggerate or fake**.

> ***Customs and Excise Commissioners v Barclays Bank plc* [2007] 1 AC 181, [31] (Lord Hoffmann):** "In the case of **personal or physical injury, reasonable foreseeability of harm is usually enough**, in accordance with the principle in *Donoghue v Stevenson*, to generate a duty of care. **In the case of economic loss, something more is needed.**"

- See also ***So Kai Hau v YSK2 Engineering Co Ltd* [2018] HKEC 2142 (Bharwaney J)**.

**Caution: policy should not lightly defeat a claim**
> ***Spring v Guardian Assurance plc* [1995] 2 AC 296, 326 (Lord Lowry):** "…the courts… ought to **think very carefully before resorting to public policy considerations which will defeat a claim** that ex hypothesi is a perfectly good cause of action… public policy should be invoked **only in clear cases in which the potential harm to the public is incontestable**… determined on **tangible grounds instead of on mere generalities** and… **the burden of proof lies on those who assert that the court should not enforce a liability which prima facie exists**."

**Policy changes over time**
> ***Michael*, [161] (Lord Kerr):** "…**what one group of judges felt was the correct policy answer in 2009, should not bind another group of judges, even as little as five years later**."

- **Example: advocates' immunity.** *Rondel v Worsley* [1969] 1 AC 191 gave advocates immunity. ***Arthur JS Hall & Co v Simons* [2002] 1 AC 615** declined to follow it.

> ***HKSAR v Hung Chan Wa* (2006) 9 HKCFAR 614, [31] (Li CJ):** this "did not mean that *Rondel v Worsley* was wrongly decided. Rather the question was **reconsidered over three decades later in the light of changes in the law of negligence, the functioning of the legal profession, the administration of justice and public perceptions**."

### 18.5 The main policy arguments for denying or limiting a DOC

| | Argument | Explanation | Authority |
|---|---|---|---|
| **(a)** | **Floodgates of litigation** | If the DOC is expanded, **how many new cases will suddenly appear**? | ***Caparo*** |
| **(b)** | **Indeterminate liability** | **Fairness to potential Ds.** Businesses that **can't predict the extent of liability can't calculate costs or plan**. That makes a less favourable business environment, and unpredictable expansion of DOC **may deter investment**. | ***Ultramares Corp v Touche* 255 NY 170, 179 (Cardozo J):** "liability in an **indeterminate amount** for an **indeterminate time** to an **indeterminate class**" |
| **(c)** | **(Excessively) defensive practice** | Liability may **change how officials behave**. E.g. police may focus on **avoiding litigation** instead of investigating crime. | ***Hill*, 63 (Lord Keith):** "…the imposition of liability may lead to the exercise of a function being carried on in a **detrimentally defensive frame of mind**." |
| **(d)** | **Financial burden on the public** | If the police lose a claim, **who pays?** The **Government**, which means **the public**. | ***Michael*, [122] (Lord Toulson):** compensation and claim costs "would have to come either from the **police budget, with corresponding reduction of spending on other services**, or from an **increased burden on the public**…" |

## 19. Public authorities and the police

### 19.1 Public authorities: general principles
- PAs (including **police and fire services**) have **statutory powers and duties to protect the public from harm**.
- **But breach of a statutory duty does not automatically lead to liability in negligence.** Legislation gives PAs public powers to protect the public, but **failing to use them is not by itself a tort**.
- PAs **owe tort duties to protect under the same principles as other defendants**. They are liable for **ordinary torts** like anyone else, e.g. a council driver who carelessly runs someone over.
- **Statutes can alter this position**, either by **imposing a statutory duty of care beyond the common law** or by **excluding common law liability**.

### 19.2 When is a PA liable for failing to protect?
**Where PAs fail in their statutory duty, common law liability is exceptional.** It can arise where:

| Exception | Authority |
|---|---|
| **(a) The PA creates the danger** | ***Dorset Yacht*** (§16.2) |
| **(b) The PA assumes responsibility.** This **requires more than having the statutory power or duty to act**. E.g. the police **specifically say they will cover something**. | ***N v Poole BC*** (no assumption of responsibility on the assumed facts) **[CHECK citation: the slides give "[2018] UKSC 18", but the case is usually cited [2019] UKSC 25, [2020] AC 780]** |
| **(c) Interference principle:** the PA acts in a way that "**puts off or prevents someone else from taking steps to protect the claimant from harm**", e.g. suggesting that others need not worry | ***Tindall v Chief Constable of Thames Valley Police* [2024] UKSC 33** (not satisfied on the facts) |

### 19.3 The police: no DOC for negligent investigation or suppression of crime
**Why not?**
1. **No proximity.** The police have **no proximity with the general public**, unless an exception applies.
2. **Public policy:**
   - **Defensive policing:** police might focus on **protecting themselves** from lawsuits rather than **doing their job** (*Hill*, Lord Keith).
   - **Diversion of resources** to litigation.
   - **Counter-argument:** liability might **force the police to do a better job**. **Response:** courts **should not second-guess the police's operational priorities**.

**Serial killer example (*Hill v CC West Yorkshire* [1989] AC 53):** the police were investigating a serial killer (the Yorkshire Ripper). A victim was killed. **Her family could not sue.**

**The same policy concerns are shared in HK:**
> ***Liu Mei Huei v Government of HKSAR* [2016] 2 HKLRD 249 (Chu JA):** "In *Hill*… the House of Lords held that the police owed the victim who was killed by a serial killer no duty of care not only because she was **only a member of public who had no relationship of proximity with the police** but also because of **public policy considerations**… once a duty of care had been imposed… they **might become defensive to protect themselves**… This is not in the public interest. Moreover, once the police are dragged into litigation, they will have to **divert the time and resources** that should have been used on performing their duties…"

**Even calling the police for help is not enough.** Where police negligence **allows a third party to harm the victim**, a DOC tends to be **denied for policy reasons**:
- ***Smith v CC Sussex Police*; *Van Colle v CC Hertfordshire Police* [2009] 1 AC 225** (conjoined)
- ***Michael v CC South Wales* [2015] AC 1732** (a 999 call)

**But a DOC is owed where police negligence *directly causes* injury.** In ***Robinson* [2018] UKSC 4**, officers arresting a suspect **knocked over an elderly passer-by**. That was a **positive act**, so ordinary principles apply (unless a statute excludes liability).

| **Police fail to protect P from a third party** | **Police directly injure P** |
|---|---|
| **Generally no DOC** (*Hill*, *Smith/Van Colle*, *Michael*, *Liu Mei Huei*) | **DOC owed** (*Robinson*) |
| Exceptions: **assumption of responsibility**, **creating the danger**, **interference** (§19.2) | |

## 20. Omissions: no general duty to confer a benefit

**General rule:** there is **no duty of care to confer benefits on another**, e.g. **to protect a person from harm**.

| **Acts causing harm** | **Failure to protect or confer a benefit** |
|---|---|
| A DOC **will generally arise**. | **Generally no DOC.** |
| E.g. careless driving that injures someone | E.g. you **see a person drowning in a lake** and do nothing. **No liability.** |

- **Exceptions** match §19.2: **creating the danger**, **control over a third party** (*Dorset Yacht*), **assumption of responsibility**, and **interference with others' protection**. **[CHECK: whether any further exceptions were taught]**
- Omissions cases require **close proximity** (§18.3).
- In exam answers, **classify D's conduct first**: is D **making things worse** (an act) or **failing to make things better** (an omission)?

## 21. Scope of duty

### 21.1 The idea
- Sometimes **a DOC between P and D is established by precedent, but its scope (extent) is contentious**.
- So besides asking **whether a DOC exists**, ask **what the duty covers**. This is **another way of limiting negligence claims**.
- **Scope depends on the facts of each case**, assessed **by reference to the kind of damage**.

> ***Caparo*, 627 (Lord Bridge):** "It is always necessary to **determine the scope of the duty by reference to the kind of damage** from which A must take care to save B harmless."
> - *Caparo* itself: even if an **auditor** owed a DOC to a **shareholder**, it **did not extend to losses from buying additional shares** in reliance on the audit report. In doing so the shareholder acted as an **ordinary market participant**, to whom **no DOC** is owed.

- **Bouncer example (lecture):** a club requires customers to wear leather, and the bouncer turns someone away because he doesn't like their leather, or thinks there isn't enough of it. **[CHECK: the point this example was making. It seems to show that an existing rule or duty doesn't automatically cover every situation it might be stretched to.]**

### 21.2 *Darnley* [2019] AC 831: scope extended
- **Issue:** does the **established healthcare provider–patient duty**, which one might think covers only **actual medical services**, extend to **not giving misleading information** (a wrong waiting time) that causes physical injury?

> **[16] (Lord Lloyd-Jones):** "…the scope of the duty to take reasonable care not to act in such a way as foreseeably to cause such a patient to sustain physical injury **clearly extends to a duty to take reasonable care not to provide misleading information which may foreseeably cause physical injury**."

### 21.3 *Mitchell v Glasgow City Council* [2009] UKHL 11, [2009] 1 AC 874: scope not extended
- **Established duty:** a **landlord owes duties to its tenants**, e.g. to provide a **liveable property**.
- **Issue:** does that duty extend to **warning a tenant about a violent fellow tenant**?
- **Facts:** the council knew the other tenant had **previously threatened his neighbour** (Mitchell). The council met the tenant about his behaviour and possible **eviction**, and he became **very angry**. He then **killed Mitchell**. Mitchell's family sued in negligence for **failing to warn** him. **[CHECK facts against the case]**
- **Held: no DOC.**
  - **Foreseeability of harm is not enough** to create a duty to protect against a **third party's criminal act** (the omissions rule, §20).
  - **FJR:** a duty to **warn neighbours** would **deter landlords from acting**, e.g. trying to evict. *(The lecturer questioned how convincing this argument is.)*
  - The **established landlord–tenant category does not extend this far**.
- See also ***James-Bowen v Commissioner of Police of the Metropolis* [2018] UKSC 40**.
- **Lesson:** always ask **both**: **is there a DOC?** and **does the scope of the DOC cover these facts?**

### 21.4 *Pickersgill v Riley* [2004] UKPC 14: commercial setting
> **[7] (Lord Scott):** "It is plain that when a **solicitor** is instructed by a client to act in a transaction, **a duty of care arises**. But it is also plain that **the scope of that duty of care is variable**. It will… depend, first and foremost, **upon the content of the instructions** given to the solicitor by the client. It will depend also on **the particular circumstances of the case**. It is a duty that it is **not helpful to try to describe in the abstract**."

- **Facts:** Mr Riley was a **sophisticated businessperson**, **not completely in the solicitor's hands**, and **should have been fully aware of the risks**.
- **Financial loss** case, where courts are **much more sceptical** of a DOC.
- **Held:** a solicitor has **no automatic duty to protect a client from their own commercial missteps**. The duty **may be higher for an unsophisticated or vulnerable client**, and **lower for a sophisticated one**.
- 💡 **Exam tip:** in a **solicitor negligence** question, it is **not enough to say "established category" and move on**. Consider **whether the duty covers these facts**.

### 21.5 Where does scope of duty belong?
> ***Khan v Meadows* [2021] UKSC 21, [38] (Lord Hodge and Lord Sales):** "In our view it is **often helpful to ask the scope of duty question before turning to questions as to breach of duty and causation**. It asks: '**what, if any, risks of harm did the defendant owe a duty of care to protect the claimant against?**' The question is appropriately asked and answered at this stage, if it can be, in relation for example to the circumstances in which loss has been incurred, as in *Caparo*… or in relation to claims resulting from **omissions**…"

- **Overlap with causation**, in particular the **"duty nexus" question**: is **all the loss claimed the consequence of the risk against which D had to take care?** See ***South Australia Asset Management Corp v York Montague Ltd* (SAAMCO) [1997] AC 191**; ***Manchester Building Society v Grant Thornton UK LLP* [2021] UKSC 20**. *(Relevant again in Weeks 7–9.)*
- 💡 **Where you raise scope of duty (under duty or causation) matters less than *that* you raise it.** Cite *Khan v Meadows*.

## 22. Duty of care: putting it together

**Summary (from the slides):**
- **If the facts are covered by precedent**, there is **no need for the *Caparo* three-stage approach**: **follow the precedent**.
- **In novel scenarios**, where there is no established category, the applicability of an established category is contentious, or **the scope of an established duty is unclear**, decide the DOC **incrementally** by:
  - considering **possible analogies with established categories**; and
  - using the ***Caparo* three-stage approach** to **guide and structure** the analysis (foreseeability, proximity, FJR).

**Recommended order in an exam answer:**
1. **Precedent.** Is there an **established category** of DOC (or of no DOC)? (*Robinson*)
2. If not, or if borderline, reason **incrementally by analogy**, guided by ***Caparo*** (*Luen Hing Fat* in HK).
3. **Scope.** Does the duty cover **this kind of harm on these facts**? (*Caparo*, *Darnley*, *Mitchell*, *Pickersgill*, *Khan v Meadows*)
4. **Special rules:** **omissions** (§20), **public authorities and police** (§19), and **type of loss** (PEL, psychiatric harm) (§14).

> ⚠️ **Always deal with duty before breach.** Only once a DOC is established do you move to the **standard of care**, which belongs to **breach**.

# PART C — NEGLIGENCE: BREACH OF DUTY

> Breach is **usually the most obvious question in a tort problem**. Students often jump straight to it, but **always establish duty first** (Part B).

## 23. Breach: the two questions

| | Question | Type | Explanation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **What is the standard of care?** | **Question of law** | What would the **reasonable person** in D's position have done? |
| **2** | **Did D's conduct fall short of that standard?** | **Question of fact** | Compare what D **actually did** with the standard. **If D fell short, there is a breach; if D met it, there is no breach.** |

**Simple example: road accident**
- **DOC:** established, since motorists owe a DOC to other road users (*Robinson* [26]).
- **Q1 (law):** the standard is that of a **reasonably competent driver**.
- **Q2 (fact):** in this case, did D's driving **fall short** of that standard?

**Points to note**
- The **facts play a huge role in the second question.**
- **Pleadings:** P must set out the **particulars of negligence**. These are the **exact acts or omissions** complained of, stated **specifically**.
- **Judge D fairly, prospectively.** Put yourself, as far as possible, **at the time of the accident**, **without knowing it would happen**. **No hindsight.**

---

## 24. The objective standard and the reasonable person

### 24.1 What "objective" means
- **Not** objective in a literal or philosophical sense.
- It means the standard is judged **not from D's own perspective** but from that of **a reasonable person**.

### 24.2 The classical formulation: *Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks Co* (1856)
- **Facts:** a **fire plug in a water main** was allegedly **negligently installed**. In an **unprecedentedly severe winter frost**, it leaked and **flooded P's house**.
- **Held: not liable**, even though the water came from the fire plug. The frost was **so exceptional**, a **freak act of nature**, that a reasonable person would not have guarded against it.
- *Extra:* the classic definition of negligence (Alderson B) is "**the omission to do something which a reasonable man, guided upon those considerations which ordinarily regulate the conduct of human affairs, would do, or doing something which a prudent and reasonable man would not do**". **[CHECK whether you need it verbatim]**

### 24.3 Who is the reasonable person?
- **Not a real person.** It is a **fictional, judicial construct of the common law**.
- **Not the same as the average or ordinary person.**
  - Average people sometimes act unreasonably, e.g. **checking their phone while driving**.
  - **A reasonable person would never check their phone while driving.**
  - So **what the average person does is not what the reasonable person does**.
- **Careful and prudent, and does the proper thing.** **Not perfect, but not irresponsible.** Think of **the average person always on their best behaviour**.
- Traditionally **"the man on the Clapham omnibus"**. **[CHECK source: usually *Hall v Brooklands Auto-Racing Club* [1933]]**
- **"Free both from over-apprehension and from over-confidence"**: ordinary people have strengths and weaknesses, but the reasonable person is **"just right"**. **[CHECK source: usually *Glasgow Corporation v Muir* [1943] AC 448, Lord Macmillan]**

### 24.4 *Bolton v Stone* [1951] AC 850: no duty to eliminate every risk
- **Facts:** a cricket ball was **hit out of the ground** and injured P on the road outside. P sued the **cricket club** in **negligence** and **nuisance**.
- **Held (HL): no breach.**
  - A ball leaving the ground was **possible**, but the **frequency and likelihood were so tiny** that a reasonable person in the club's position could **justifiably disregard the risk**.
  - **Ordinary care does not mean eliminating all risks.** That would impose a **huge burden** and **unrealistic measures**.
  - "**An ordinary man does not take precautions against every foreseeable risk.**" He takes **certain precautions, but not all.**
- Link: **optimal accident prevention** (§7.4).

---

## 25. How objective is the objective standard?

### 25.1 The general rule

| Direction | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Lowered?** | **Only for children**, in general. **Not** for **physical or mental incapacity**. |
| **Raised?** | **More often**, for people **professing or possessing special skills**. **[To be covered in later notes]** |

**Why no lowering for incapacity?**
- A reasonable person who is **blind, deaf or has trouble moving** will **take that into account in everything they do**, and must **modify their behaviour** accordingly.
- A **blind adult** can take account of being blind. An **8-year-old cannot take account of being 8**.
- E.g. a **98-year-old who can barely see** gets **no discount**.

> 💡 **Exam tip:** ask **in what situations the standard would be lowered or raised**, and whether D falls into one.

### 25.2 Inexperience: *Nettleship v Weston* [1971] 2 QB 691
- **Issue:** should a **learner driver** be judged by a **lower standard**?
- **Held: no.** A **reasonably certain standard of care** is preferable.
  - **Anyone operating a car** must meet the standard of the **competent and experienced driver**.
  - There is **no "brand new driver" standard**.
- *Link:* **compulsory motor insurance** (§10.2) is part of why courts accept this.

### 25.3 Children: the standard is lowered (objectively)
- **The exception** to the general rule against lowering the standard.
- The standard is lowered **objectively**: **not what this child knew**, but what **a reasonable child of D's age and experience** would foresee.
- This is tricky, because **children of the same age develop very differently**.

***McHale v Watson* [1966] ALR 513 (High Court of Australia)**
- A **12-year-old boy** threw a sharp object that glanced off a post and injured a girl.
- **Held:** the standard is that of a **reasonable child of the same age, intelligence and experience**, **not an adult**, applied **objectively**. The court does not examine this boy's actual background; it asks what a **typical boy of that age and type** would foresee.
- **In favour of Watson (D). No breach**, because he met the standard of a reasonable 12-year-old.
- **Exception to the exception:** a child **driving a car** is **held to the adult standard**. **[CHECK: authority]**

***Mullin v Richards* [1998] 1 WLR 1304 (CA)** **[CHECK against the breach slides]**
- **Facts:** two **15-year-old schoolgirls** were **play-fencing with plastic rulers**. One ruler **snapped**, and a fragment **partially blinded** one girl.
- **Held:** they were **close to adulthood but still children**, so the standard is that of a **reasonable 15-year-old**. Ruler fencing was **common**, and this was a **freak accident**. A reasonable 15-year-old **would not have foreseen serious injury**. **No breach.**
