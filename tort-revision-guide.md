# PART A — FOUNDATIONS OF TORT

## 1. Degrees of culpability

**Key point:** Of the "fault" terms, **negligence is the only one that is itself a tort**. *Recklessness* and the others are **states of mind / degrees of fault**, not causes of action.

- In a negligence claim the question is simply: **was the tortfeasor negligent?**
- **Where recklessness matters:**
  1. **Trespass to the person**
  2. **The tort of harassment**
  3. **Torts defined by statute**, where the statute sets the required mental state
  4. **At the damages stage**, when assessing punitive/exemplary damages

**Recklessness and damages**
- **Punitive / exemplary damages** are awarded **to punish D** over and above what is needed to compensate P.
- They may be appropriate where D was **genuinely reckless as to P's wellbeing**, e.g. D disregarded safety for the sake of profits.
- Example from lecture: the **McDonald's hot coffee case**. **[CASE TBC]** *(likely* Liebeck v McDonald's Restaurants *(1994, USA). US case; punitive damages.)*

## 2. What is tort law doing? Aims and functions

**Big picture:** tort law **intervenes directly in the real world** by **shifting financial loss** from the person who suffered it to the person who caused it.

### Why do we do this?

| Aim | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Compensation** | The person who caused the harm should, in fairness, pay the innocent person who was injured. |
| **Deterrence** | Making negligence costly discourages risky behaviour and helps prevent future accidents. |
| **Vindication of rights** | Sends a message that people's rights matter. |
| **Appeasement** *(Glanville Williams, Welsh legal scholar)* | Gives people a way to settle grievances **without private vengeance** or physical retaliation. |

### Deterrence in more detail
- **Specific deterrence:** aimed at the **individual defendant**.
- **General deterrence:** aimed at the **wider public**.
- **How it works for businesses:**
  - Liability **raises the cost of negligence**. Businesses factor in the cost of lawsuits and of harming someone, so they become unwilling to take the risk.
  - Costs are often **passed on to consumers** (e.g. food prices rise by X% to cover lawsuit costs).
  - If the cost is high enough, the risky behaviour stops.
- **Measuring whether it works:**
  - Deterrence aims to **prevent a repeat** of the activity.
  - **Every repeated tort shows that deterrence has failed** in that case.
  - Why might it fail? D **buys insurance**, or **payouts are too low**. It may work, but it is hard to prove.

## 3. Economic perspectives

### 3.1 Loss shifting vs loss spreading

| | **Loss shifting** | **Loss spreading (distribution)** |
|---|---|---|
| Idea | The victim usually can't afford the harm, so shift the loss to the **party who caused it**. | Spread the loss **across many people** rather than one D. |
| Examples | A standard tort claim, P v D. | **"Tort tax"**: a coffee chain raises prices to fund lawsuits, so the loss spreads to all coffee drinkers. **Insurance.** **New Zealand**: a **government agency pays compensation** instead of P suing D, spreading the cost across society. **Some HK Ordinances** do this directly. **[CHECK which Ordinances]** |
| Linked to | **Corrective justice** | **Distributive justice** |

### 3.2 Administrative / legal efficiency
- Is the tort system an efficient way to **resolve disputes and get people compensated**, or is it a hassle?
- Would **other methods** (e.g. a no-fault scheme) do better? The tort system **could be reformed**.

### 3.3 Economic efficiency
- If tort liability costs are passed on to consumers, does the system actually produce the **optimal level of safety**?

### 3.4 Law and economics
- **Assumption:** people are **rational actors** who respond to **financial incentives**. They do things that cost little and avoid things that cost a lot.
- Tort liability creates **incentives to invest in safety**.
- **But tort does not aim to eliminate all harm.** It **balances costs and benefits**.
  - **Car example:** you could eliminate about 99% of accidents by designing cars that can't exceed 10 km/h. That would **destroy the car industry**, and neither car owners nor society would want it.
- **Optimal accident prevention:**
  - The aim is **optimal** prevention, **not** preventing every accident.
  - Precautions should be taken **up to the point where the cost of an additional precaution exceeds the expected harm it prevents**.
  - Tort law **expects that accidents will happen**.
  - Key question for a company: is it cheaper to **prevent the accident** or to **pay compensation**?
  - *Extra (not from notes):* this is often expressed as the **Learned Hand formula (B < P × L)**. It may come up again under **breach**.

## 4. Theoretical perspectives

### 4.1 Rights-based theory
- A **breach of a duty owed to others** gives rise to a **remedy** (usually damages).
- **A claim exists even without damage.** The wrong is the **infringement of P's right**.
  - **Trespass to land:** crossing onto a neighbour's property, even by accident.
  - **Battery:** punching someone, or any unwanted touching, is a tort even if it causes no real injury.

### 4.2 Utilitarianism
- A school of philosophy that **does not believe in rights**. Only **pleasure and pain** exist; the goal is to **increase pleasure and decrease pain**.
- Even without individual rights, utilitarians still **support a tort system**, because it **maximises overall welfare and economic efficiency**.

### 4.3 Corrective vs distributive justice

| | **Corrective justice** | **Distributive justice** |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Justice **between two individuals** | Justice **across society** |
| Idea | Put P back in the position they were in before the tort. Paying damages **restores moral equality** between the parties. | **Fair distribution / allocation of resources, risks and burdens** in society |
| Mechanism | **Loss shifting** | **Loss spreading / distribution** |

### 4.4 Fault-based liability
- Shifting loss through tort **generally depends on fault**.
- **Exception: strict liability torts.** No fault needs to be found. The risks of these activities are so high that P should recover **even if D met the standard of reasonable care**.

---

## 5. Limits of compensation

**Only *legally recognised* losses are compensated.** Not every loss someone suffers can be recovered through tort.

- **Psychiatric harm** and **pure economic loss** are **not recoverable in many situations**, for **policy reasons**.
  - **Floodgates:** allowing one claim sets a precedent that would let many others in, and liability becomes too open-ended.
- **Pure economic loss:** a simple **loss of money** caused by someone's act.
  - Generally recoverable **only where P relied on advice from someone who assumed responsibility** (see *Hedley Byrne*, §12.1).
- **Simple grief:** if a sibling is injured and you feel sad or angry but have **no recognised mental illness**, courts generally **refuse** recovery.

## 6. The impact of insurance

### 6.1 Types
- **First-party insurance:** **indemnifies the insured** for their own loss **without reference to the tort system**.
  - E.g. your property is damaged, so you claim on your property insurance.
  - The insurer then has a **right of subrogation**: it **steps into the victim's shoes** and can sue the tortfeasor itself.
  - The victim doesn't have to take on the risk of a tort case. **Insurers may be the real parties suing** on the victim's behalf.
- **Liability (third-party) insurance:** if you crash into someone, **your insurer pays** the damages for you.
  - **Often compulsory** (e.g. motor insurance), because people cause accidents they can't afford to pay for. **[CHECK: HK example, e.g. Motor Vehicles Insurance (Third Party Risks) Ordinance (Cap 272)]**

### 6.2 Theoretical problems
- **Does compulsory insurance undermine personal responsibility?** If D is insured and the insurer pays, **where is the corrective justice?** This is a theoretical question to consider in essays.
- **Insurance solves the most important practical problem: compensation.**

### 6.3 Practical effect on judges
- Judges tend to be **more willing to find a DOC where insurance is mandatory**.
- Courts say insurance "doesn't matter", but in practice **mandatory insurance makes a finding of DOC more likely**.

## 7. Parties: who can sue and be sued; immunities

### 7.1 Who can sue?
Anyone with **legal capacity**, including:
- **Newborns**
- **The estate of a deceased person** (it can sue even though the person has died)
- **Spouses.** E.g. a husband drives negligently and his wife, a passenger, is injured. **The wife can sue her husband.**

### 7.2 Who can be sued?
**Any wrongdoer with legal capacity**, including:
- **Children.** They have **no absolute immunity** in tort.
- **Corporations.** E.g. something falls from a building onto you on the pavement, so you sue the corporation that owns the building.
- **The Government.** E.g. you slip and fall while visiting LegCo, so you sue the relevant government party.

### 7.3 Immunities
**Immunity** is a limit on who can be sued. It is **usually not available**, but can arise by **statute** or at **common law**.

| Type | Explanation | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Statutory immunity** | The legislature bars lawsuits against certain parties. | **Legislators.** The immunity covers **only their statements**, to promote free speech and debate in the legislature. **[CHECK: HK source, e.g. Legislative Council (Powers and Privileges) Ordinance (Cap 382)]** **Foreign states.** |
| **Common law immunity** | Works through **no DOC being owed**: no DOC means no claim. | **Police:** no DOC to the public for **failure to investigate or prevent crime**. **[CASE TBC]** *(likely* Hill v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire *[1989])* |

> *Extra (not from notes):* in *Robinson* (§12.5) the UKSC said the police rule is **not a blanket immunity**. Police can owe a DOC for **positive acts** that cause injury, but generally not for **failing to protect** the public from third parties.

## 8. Remedies

### 8.1 Damages: the standard remedy
- **"Damage"** (singular) = the **harm or loss** suffered.
- **"Damages"** (plural) = the **monetary award**.

| Type | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Special damages** | **Quantifiable** losses already incurred, e.g. **receipts**. |
| **General damages** | **Non-quantifiable** losses and **future** losses. |
| **Nominal damages** | P has suffered **no real damage** (e.g. trespass). A **very small sum** (e.g. HK$100) recognises the infringement of the right. |
| **Restitutionary damages** | Strip D of an **unjust gain**. |
| **Aggravated damages** | A **type of compensation** for **aggravated harm**, e.g. extra distress caused by D's conduct. |
| **Exemplary (punitive) damages** | **Not compensation.** They **punish D** and **make a statement**. Linked to **recklessness** (§1). |

> **Aggravated vs exemplary:** aggravated damages **compensate P**; exemplary damages **punish D**.

### 8.2 Equitable remedies
- **Non-monetary** remedies.
- Used where **damages would not give adequate relief**, e.g. a **nuisance** claim where P wants an **injunction** to stop the activity.
- May require **judicial monitoring** (court supervision of compliance).

# PART B — NEGLIGENCE: DUTY OF CARE

## 9. Negligence: overview and exam checklist

- **The dominant modern tort.**
- Imposes liability on D for **carelessly causing harm** to P.
- Focus is on **fault**: **falling short of an objective standard of care**.

### ✅ The exam checklist (marks are awarded for each component)

| # | Element | Question |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Duty of care** | Was a DOC **owed** by D to P? |
| 2 | **Breach** | Did D **breach** that duty? |
| 3 | **Factual causation** | Did the breach **in fact** cause the harm? |
| 4 | **Legal causation / remoteness** | Was the harm **so remote** that it would be unfair to impose liability? |
| 5 | **Damage** | Did P suffer **actionable damage**? |
| 6 | **Defences** | Does **any defence** apply? |

> ⚠️ **Always go through every step.** **Always state whether any defences apply, even if none do** ("No defences appear to apply on these facts").

**Notes on structure:**
- **There are two types of causation:** **factual** and **legal**.
- **Scope of duty** is closely **related to Question 1 (duty)**, but it is sometimes placed **near the end (as Question 6)**. **Placement is not universally agreed**; different judges and scholars put it in different places. **[CHECK: likely a reference to the six-question model in *Meadows v Khan* [2021] UKSC 21 / *Manchester Building Society v Grant Thornton* [2021] UKSC 20]**

## 10. Duty of care as a legal filter

**Purpose of DOC:** it **acts as a legal filter** that **sets the boundaries of negligence liability**. Once a claim gets past DOC, the other elements can be worked through more clearly.

**Three factors that affect whether a DOC exists:**

1. **Type of conduct: acts vs omissions**
   - **Omissions are unlikely to give rise to a DOC.**
   - *Example:* you walk past a baby drowning. At common law you **have no DOC to rescue it**, so the parents cannot sue you in negligence.
   - *Related (illegality):* two drug dealers are packing drugs and one accidentally stabs the other. **No DOC**, because they were **engaged in an illegal enterprise**. **[CASE TBC]**
2. **Type of loss**
   - E.g. **psychiatric harm** from seeing a close relative severely injured. A claim is often unlikely to succeed because the court holds **no DOC is owed to you** for that type of loss. **[CASE TBC]** *(likely* Alcock v Chief Constable of South Yorkshire *[1992])*
   - Also **pure economic loss** (§5).
3. **Type of person / relationship**
   - E.g. P **voluntarily participated** in certain activities. **[CHECK: examples given]**

## 11. *Donoghue v Stevenson* (1932) and the neighbour principle

### 11.1 Before 1932
- A DOC was owed **only in established categories of relationship** between P and D.
  - E.g. **one party invited another onto their property**.
- *Donoghue* **opened the gates**, leading to a **dramatic expansion of negligence**. After 1932 the DOC was located **much more broadly across society**.

### 11.2 Facts and significance
- P (Mrs Donoghue) had **no contract** and **no direct relationship** with the café owner or the manufacturer, because **her friend paid for the drink**.
- **Under contract law she could not sue.**
- She argued that, contract or no contract, the **manufacturer failed in its duty to keep its products safe**.

### 11.3 Lord Atkin's neighbour principle (**learn verbatim**)

> "You must take **reasonable care** to avoid **acts or omissions** which you can **reasonably foresee** would be likely to injure your **neighbour**."

> "Who, then, in law is my neighbour? … **persons who are so closely and directly affected by my act that I ought reasonably to have them in contemplation as being so affected** when I am directing my mind to the acts or omissions which are called in question."

- **Reasonableness is the essence of negligence.** You avoid liability if you took **reasonable care**.
- **In modern terms:** your neighbour is **anyone you should have in mind** when deciding whether to do or not do something, **because you would expect them to be closely and directly affected** by it.
- *Example:* when deciding whether to drive especially fast to the hospital, consider **everyone closely or directly affected by your driving**.
- **Who your neighbour is changes for every act or omission.**
- The principle applied **to everyone under British law**. It is the **general conception of relationships giving rise to a DOC**.

### 11.4 Broad vs narrow ratio

| | **Broad ratio** | **Narrow ratio** |
|---|---|---|
| Content | The **neighbour principle**: a general test for DOC | A **manufacturer owes a DOC to the ultimate consumer** of its products |
| Significance | Foundation of the modern law of negligence | **Foundation of the modern commercial economy.** Consumers can **trust that products meet a standard of reasonable care** (e.g. Coca-Cola, crisps). This extends to **non-food items**, e.g. earbuds bought online. |

## 12. Development after 1932

After *Donoghue* the courts had to work out **the limits of the new principle**. The concern was **opening the floodgates** to negligence claims everywhere, so they **refined and narrowed** the duty using factors such as **reasonable foreseeability** and **proximity**.

### 12.1 *Hedley Byrne v Heller* [1964]: pure economic loss
- **Pure economic loss** (loss of money) is **generally not recoverable** in negligence…
- …**unless there is a special relationship** between P and the adviser, and the adviser was **negligent** (e.g. did no proper research). P can then sue.

### 12.2 *Home Office v Dorset Yacht Co* [1970]: omissions and third-party acts
- **Facts:** borstal trainees escaped while under the supervision of Home Office officers and **damaged a yacht**. The yacht owner sued the **Home Office**, since the trainees had no money and the Government had the resources.
- **Issue:** did the Home Office (whose officers were supposed to supervise the boys) owe a **DOC to the yacht owner**?
- **Held: DOC owed.**
  - There was a **special relationship between the officers and the boys** (control).
  - The Home Office did not act. It **failed to supervise**, which is an **omission**. It should still have **foreseen the possible impact on its "neighbours"**, such as the yacht owner.
  - **Liability for failing to prevent third parties causing harm.** The boys' acts being criminal **does not get D off the hook**.
- **Public policy:** should the Home Office have **immunity** here? The court looked at the impact of imposing a duty.

**Policy vs operational distinction (public authorities)**

| **Allocation of resources / policy decisions** | **Negligent execution of duties** |
|---|---|
| **Non-justiciable.** You **cannot sue the Government over a policy decision you disagree with**. | **Reviewable.** A claim **can proceed**. |
| *E.g.* you trip in a HK park and argue "if the Government hadn't spent so much helping wealthy people, I wouldn't have tripped". **This fails**: it is an administrative/political choice. | *E.g.* a **particular failure of maintenance** that caused the trip. **This may proceed.** |

### 12.3 *Anns v Merton LBC* [1978]: the two-stage test
1. Is there a **sufficient relationship of proximity** (neighbourhood) between P and D?
2. If so, are there **public policy considerations that negate or restrict** the duty?

> *Extra (not from notes):* *Anns* was later **departed from** (*Murphy v Brentwood DC* [1991]) because it was seen as too expansive. **[CHECK whether covered]**

### 12.4 *Caparo Industries v Dickman* [1990]: the three-stage approach
Three questions, **similar to *Anns***:
1. Was the **harm reasonably foreseeable** to P?
2. Was there a **sufficient relationship of proximity** between P and D?
3. Is it **fair, just and reasonable** to impose a DOC?
   - These three words call for **public policy considerations**. The court looks **beyond the two parties** to the **legal system and society as a whole**.

### 12.5 *Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire* [2018] UKSC 4: the proper use of *Caparo*
- Explains and **clarifies how *Caparo* should be used**. People had **misunderstood** it as a universal test.
- ***Caparo* is for new or novel situations.**
- **Where the law is clear** that a particular relationship or recurrent factual situation gives rise to a DOC (or does not), **there is no occasion to resort to *Caparo***. Just **follow precedent**, unless the case asks the court to **re-examine** the established law.

## 13. The current approach to establishing a DOC

### 13.1 Step-by-step method

```
STEP 1  Does the situation fall within an ESTABLISHED CATEGORY (DOC or no-DOC)?
          ├── YES → apply the precedent. No need for Caparo.
          └── NO / BORDERLINE → go to Step 2
STEP 2  INCREMENTAL APPROACH: reason by analogy from the closest existing precedents
STEP 3  If genuinely novel: consider the Caparo factors
          (foreseeability → proximity → fair, just and reasonable)
```

**Established categories (examples from lecture):**
- **Road users owe a DOC to other road users.** In a road-accident problem, start with DOC by citing precedent, **not *Caparo***.
- **Employer → employee.**
- **Doctor → patient.**
- **Manufacturer → consumer** (*Donoghue*).

### 13.2 Following vs modifying precedent
- **Survey the relevant precedents.** Is this a **clearly settled area** with a clear answer on DOC?
- If not, the court **cannot simply rely on established precedent**. It uses the **incremental approach**: start from established precedent, **move forward and modify the law**, looking at the issue afresh.
- **2022 case** (local court): the court held this was **not a situation where precedent clearly controlled**, so it took an incremental approach and set its own position. **[CASE TBC: name and facts]**
- **The threshold question deserves time:** does the situation **fall within an established category or not?** Cases are often **borderline**.
  - In practice (and in exams), **argue that your situation falls within the established category** where you can.

### 13.3 Worked example: the hospital receptionist
> **Facts:** a hospital receptionist **carelessly gives a wrong (too long) estimate of the waiting time**. A person in medical need **leaves** and their **injury worsens**.

1. **Established category?**
   - Doctor → patient: **yes, established**.
   - Receptionist → patient: **not a common category**.
2. **But:** the law imposes a DOC on the **healthcare provider towards the patient**, **without distinguishing between medical and non-medical staff**.
   - Once the patient is **booked into the system**, a relationship between patient and hospital exists and falls within the **established healthcare provider–patient DOC**.
   - Consider **how close the relationship is** and **whether P was booked in**.
3. **See *Darnley v Croydon Health Services NHS Trust* [2018] UKSC 50** (this hypothetical is based on it). **[CHECK / add case details]**

> 💡 **Exam technique:** where the category is borderline, **argue it both ways**. First argue it falls within an established category. Then say: "Alternatively, if this is a novel situation, the court might apply *Caparo*…" and work through the three factors.

## 14. The *Caparo* factors in detail

**Not a simple mechanical test.** The factors are **labels attached to features of different situations**, and the approach is **holistic**.

### 14.1 Foreseeability
- **Objective test:** would a reasonable person in D's position have foreseen harm?
- What must be foreseen is **the possibility of harm**, **not the exact harm** or **the exact way it came about**.
- **Airplane example:** D's negligence led to an **airplane being stolen**. D argued the event was **so unlikely and unusual** that D shouldn't be liable.
  - **Rejected.** It **does not matter that the manner of harm was unforeseeable**. It is enough that **some harm** was **to be anticipated** in the circumstances. **[CASE TBC]**

### 14.2 Proximity
- A court may find **foreseeability but no DOC because proximity is lacking**.
- **Proximity takes many forms:**

| Form | Explanation / example |
|---|---|
| **Physical closeness** | Closeness in **time and space**. |
| **Factual relationship** | *Airplane case:* proximity was found because of the **factual relationship between the (airport) authority and the airline**. **[CASE TBC]** |
| **Voluntary assumption of responsibility** | Proximity can exist where D (e.g. the **police**) **voluntarily assumes responsibility**. See the example below. |
| **Vulnerability of P** | P is in a **particularly vulnerable position** with **no way to protect themselves**, so relies on D. *Airplane case:* the airline **could not provide private security** where its planes were parked. Its **vulnerability** helped the court find proximity. |

**Police patrol example (assumption of responsibility):**
- A neighbourhood suffers many burglaries and police patrol **occasionally**. Your house is burgled and you sue the police for failing to protect it. **This fails: no proximity.** There is **no proximity between the police and every member of society**.
- **But** suppose officers on duty **volunteer**: "Don't worry, we'll watch your house for you", and the house is then burgled. Your case is **stronger**, because by **telling you they would watch the house** the police **created proximity**. **[CHECK: the notes end "but fails". Confirm whether the lecturer said this claim would ultimately still fail, and why.]**

> **Important point:** *Caparo* is **not only a limiting tool**. Although FJR is usually used to **limit** duty, **proximity can be used to create or expand a DOC**, i.e. **incremental expansion**.
> - E.g. in claims for **economic loss** or **psychiatric injury**, P may show a **clear relationship of proximity** with D.

### 14.3 Fair, just and reasonable (public policy)
- The third *Caparo* question looks at the **broader policy and social implications** of recognising (or refusing) a DOC.
- Judges consider the **public interest**: **what harm would be caused if a DOC is granted, and what harm if it is denied**.
- FJR usually **plays a bigger role where the harm is *not* foreseeable physical injury**, e.g. economic loss or psychiatric harm. See "PI vs other types of harm" below.
- It is inevitably somewhat **subjective**, because it depends on the judge's view of **what public policy requires**.
- A **major shift away from the particular parties** to **broader public policy considerations**.
- **Why?** Because of **precedent**. Once a court **establishes a new or extended DOC**, it **applies to everyone**. Later courts won't need *Caparo*, and **suddenly everyone in society owes that duty**.
- The court must **balance the whole of society's interests**: the **total detriment to the public interest** of holding a class of people liable for negligence.

**Cost–benefit analysis**
- **Benefit:** victims of negligence get a **cause of action** (compensation, and the harm done to P is recognised).
- **Cost:** a duty **affects how people act and operate**.
- The court asks **where the public interest lies** in this trade-off.
- **Criticisms / limits of judicial policymaking:**
  - Courts **lack staff** to research and produce **statistical analysis**.
  - The judge must make **some assessment of what the public interest is**, which risks **instinctual reactions**.

**Case: Kylie Grimes** **[CASE TBC: *Grimes v Hawkins* — citation]**
- **Facts:** P **dived too shallow** into D's (Mr David Hawkins's) **pool** and was seriously injured. She sued in negligence for failing to **lock the pool** and failing to put up **"no diving" signs**.
- **Held: no DOC.**
  - Foreseeable? **Yes.**
  - Proximity? **Arguably yes** (it was at his home).
  - **FJR? No.** Consider the consequences for **other pool owners in British society**. It would not be FJR to require owners to put pools out of bounds at night or prohibit adults from diving. **Adults should be aware of the risks** and **voluntarily choose** whether to take them.

**PI vs other types of harm**
- Where **physical personal injury** is involved, courts are **less likely to treat FJR as an obstacle** to a DOC.
- **Why?** Physical injury is **harder to exaggerate or fake** than **economic loss** or **psychiatric harm**.

**Courts are cautious about using policy to deny a duty**
- Courts acknowledge that **caution is required** in the use of public policy. They are cautious before **resorting to policy considerations** to deny a DOC.
- If liability **would exist under foreseeability and proximity**, there must be **clear, tangible and incontestable** policy reasons for **not allowing the claim**. **[CHECK: source. The notes say "mention under spring", possibly *Spring v Guardian Assurance plc* [1995] 2 AC 296.]**
- **Judges may revisit earlier decisions** because **public policy changes**. There might have been no DOC ten years ago but there is one now.

### 14.4 The main policy concerns (a–d)

| | Concern | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| **(a)** | **Floodgates** | If the DOC is expanded, **how many new cases will suddenly appear** in the courts? |
| **(b)** | **Indeterminate liability** | **Fairness to potential Ds.** Liability to an **indeterminate class, for an indeterminate amount, for an indeterminate time**. What are the **limits** of the expanded duty? **Business impact:** businesses must account for the risk of negligence claims. If they can't know the extent of liability, they can't **calculate expenses or plan**. That makes a **less favourable business environment**, and if courts keep expanding DOC unpredictably, **businesses may stop investing**. |
| **(c)** | **Excessively defensive practice** | How would potential negligence claims **change how officials behave**? E.g. expanding police DOC might **distract the police from investigating crime** towards **avoiding litigation**. |
| **(d)** | **Public budget** | If a claim against the police succeeds, **who pays?** The **HK Government**, which means **the public**. This puts a **financial burden on the public**. Should a DOC be imposed on this **authority** for this **victim**? |

## 15. Public authorities and the police

### 15.1 Public authorities (PAs) and statutory duties
- Many PAs have **statutory duties or powers to protect the public from harm**.
- **General rule:** a PA **will not face negligence liability merely for failing to perform its statutory duty**. **Breach of a statutory duty to protect the public does not by itself create a common law DOC.**
  - Legislation **gives PAs public powers to protect the public**, but that does **not** make them liable in negligence every time they fail to use those powers.
  - There is **no common law liability** even when a PA **fails in its statutory duty to prevent harm**.
- **However:** PAs **are subject to negligence claims** for failing to meet the **ordinary tort duties that any other defendant must follow**. E.g. a council driver who carelessly runs someone over is liable like anyone else.
- Link this to the **policy vs operational** distinction in *Dorset Yacht* (§12.2).

> *Extra (not from notes):* the leading English authorities on this point are *Stovin v Wise* [1996] AC 923 and *Gorringe v Calderdale MBC* [2004] UKHL 15. **[CHECK whether covered]**

### 15.2 The police
**General rule:** **no DOC** to members of the public to **protect them from harm caused by third parties** (e.g. to prevent crime).

**Why not?**
1. **No proximity.** The police have **no proximity with the general public**, unless an exception applies.
2. **Public policy concerns:**
   - **Defensive policing.** If police can be sued, they may focus on **protecting themselves** from lawsuits rather than **doing their job**.
   - **Counter-argument:** liability might **force them to do a better job**.
   - **Response:** courts **should not second-guess the police's operational priorities**.

**Example (serial killer):** you call the police to report a serial killer. They say "OK, we will investigate", but the victim is later killed. **The family cannot sue.** **[CASE TBC]** *(this matches* Hill v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire *[1989] AC 53, the Yorkshire Ripper case)*

**Exceptions: when the police may owe a DOC**

| Exception | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Assumption of responsibility** | The police **specifically assumed responsibility** for something, e.g. "we will cover this" or "we'll watch your house" (§14.2). |
| **Preventing P from protecting themselves** | The police **prevent someone from taking steps to protect** themselves, or **act in a way suggesting others need not worry**. |
| **Positive acts causing direct injury** | Where **police negligence directly causes injury** (a positive act, not a failure to protect), ordinary principles apply and a DOC is owed, **unless a statute makes this impossible**. See ***Robinson v CC West Yorkshire* [2018] UKSC 4**: officers arresting a suspect knocked over an elderly passer-by. |

> *Extra (not from notes):* *Michael v Chief Constable of South Wales* [2015] UKSC 2 (a 999-call case) confirms the general no-duty rule and these exceptions. **[CHECK whether covered]**

## 16. Omissions: no general duty to confer a benefit

**General rule:** there is **no DOC to confer a benefit** on another person. **Protecting someone from harm counts as conferring a benefit.**

| **Acts causing harm (misfeasance)** | **Failure to protect or benefit (nonfeasance)** |
|---|---|
| A DOC **will generally arise**. | **Generally no DOC.** |
| E.g. careless driving that injures someone. | E.g. you **see a person drowning in a lake** and do nothing. **No liability.** |

- **Exceptions** mirror §15.2: **assumption of responsibility**, **control over a third party** (*Dorset Yacht*, §12.2), **creating the danger**, and **preventing others from helping**. **[CHECK: the full list of exceptions taught]**
- In exam answers, **classify D's conduct first**: is this **making things worse** (an act) or **failing to make things better** (an omission)?

## 17. Scope of duty

### 17.1 The idea
- Besides asking **whether a DOC exists**, ask a related question: **what is the extent or scope of the duty?**
- A duty may be **established by precedent**, but it still has to **cover the kind of harm and the facts** in question.
- Scope of duty is **another way of limiting the number of negligence claims**.
- The scope of the duty **depends on the facts**, and is assessed **by reference to the kind of damage** suffered.

**Bouncer example (from lecture):** a club requires customers to wear leather, and the bouncer turns someone away because he doesn't like their leather, or thinks there isn't enough of it. **[CHECK: the point this example was making. It seems to illustrate that an existing rule or duty doesn't automatically cover every situation it might be stretched to.]**

### 17.2 *Darnley v Croydon Health Services NHS Trust* [2018] UKSC 50: scope extended
- The court held it must also consider the **scope of the duty to take reasonable care**.
- **Issue:** does the **established doctor/hospital–patient duty** extend to **not giving misleading information** (the wrong waiting time) that **may cause physical injury**?
- One could argue the duty covers only **actual medical services**, not information from reception.
- **Held:** the harm **fell within the scope of the duty owed by the hospital**. The scope of the established duty was **extended**. (Facts at §13.3.)

### 17.3 *Mitchell v Glasgow City Council* [2009] UKHL 11: scope not extended
- **Established duty:** a **landlord owes duties to its tenants**, including to provide a **liveable property** (a property law point).
- **Issue:** does the scope of that duty **extend to warning a tenant about a violent fellow tenant**?
- **Facts:** the council knew the other tenant had **previously threatened his neighbour** (Mitchell). The council met the tenant about his behaviour and the possibility of **eviction**, and he became **very angry**. He then **killed Mitchell**. Mitchell's family sued the council in common law negligence for **failing to warn** that he might be in danger. **[CHECK facts against the case]**
- **Held: no DOC.**
  - **Foreseeability of harm is not enough** to create a duty to protect against a **third party's criminal act**.
  - **FJR:** imposing a legal duty to **warn neighbours** is **not in the public interest**. It could **deter landlords from taking action** such as trying to evict. If every eviction attempt triggered a duty to warn the neighbours, landlords might do nothing. *(The lecturer questioned how convincing this argument is.)*
  - The **established category** of landlord–tenant duty **does not extend this far**.
- **Lesson:** when evaluating a problem, ask **both** questions. **Is there a DOC? Does the scope of the DOC cover these facts?**

### 17.4 *Pickersgill v Riley* [2004] UKPC 14: commercial setting
- **Solicitor–client** is an established DOC, but **what does it cover?**
- **Facts:** Mr Riley was a **sophisticated businessperson** who was **not completely in the solicitor's hands**. He was well qualified to understand the transaction and **should have been fully aware of the risks**.
- This was a **financial loss** case, and courts are **much more sceptical** about a DOC in that context.
- **Held:** solicitors have **no automatic duty to protect clients from their own commercial missteps**.
  - The duty owed by a solicitor **may be higher for an unsophisticated or vulnerable client**, and **lower for a sophisticated one**.
- **Exam tip:** in a **solicitor negligence** question, it is **not enough to say "established category" and move on**. Under *Pickersgill* you must consider **whether the duty covers these particular facts**.

### 17.5 Where does scope of duty go in an answer?
- It can be raised under **duty** or later, e.g. when discussing **causation**.
- **Where you raise it matters less than *that* you raise it.**
- Cite ***Meadows v Khan* [2021] UKSC 21** (see also §9).

## 18. Duty of care: putting it together

**Recommended order in an exam answer:**
1. **Look at precedent.** Is there an **established category** of DOC (or of no DOC)?
2. If not, or if it is borderline, apply the **factors raised by the *Caparo* test** (foreseeability, proximity, FJR).
3. **Decide whether it is appropriate to extend the DOC** incrementally to these facts.
4. **Check the scope of the duty.** Does the duty cover **this kind of harm on these facts**?
5. Consider **special rules**: **omissions** (§16), **public authorities and police** (§15), and **type of loss** (PEL, psychiatric harm).

> ⚠️ **Always deal with duty before breach.** Only once a DOC is established do you move on to the **standard of care**, which belongs to the **breach** element.

# PART C — NEGLIGENCE: BREACH OF DUTY

> Breach is **usually the most obvious question in a tort problem**. Students often jump straight to it, but **always establish duty first** (Part B).

## 19. Breach: the two questions

| | Question | Type | Explanation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **What is the standard of care?** | **Question of law** | What would the **reasonable person** in D's position have done? |
| **2** | **Did D's conduct fall short of that standard?** | **Question of fact** | Compare what D **actually did** with the standard. **If D fell short, there is a breach; if D met it, there is no breach.** |

**Simple example: road accident**
- **DOC:** established, since road users owe a DOC to other road users (§13.1).
- **Q1 (law):** the standard is that of a **reasonably competent driver**.
- **Q2 (fact):** in this case, did D's driving **fall short** of that standard?

**Points to note**
- The **facts of the case play a huge role in the second question.**
- **Pleadings:** P must set out the **particulars of negligence**. These are the **exact acts or omissions** complained of, stated **specifically**.
- **Judge D fairly, prospectively.** Put yourself, as far as possible, **at the time of the accident**, **without knowing the accident would happen**. **Do not use hindsight.**

---

## 20. The objective standard and the reasonable person

### 20.1 What "objective" means
- It does **not** mean objective in a literal or philosophical sense.
- It means the standard is judged **not from D's own perspective**, but from the perspective of **a reasonable person**.

### 20.2 The classical formulation: *Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks Co* (1856)
- **Facts:** a **fire plug in a water main** had allegedly been **negligently installed**. During an **unprecedentedly severe winter frost**, it leaked and **P's house was flooded**.
- **Held: not liable**, even though the water damage came from the fire plug.
  - The cold was **so exceptional**, a **freak act of nature**, that a reasonable person would not have guarded against it.
- *Extra (not from notes):* this case gives the classic definition. Negligence is "**the omission to do something which a reasonable man, guided upon those considerations which ordinarily regulate the conduct of human affairs, would do, or doing something which a prudent and reasonable man would not do**" (Alderson B). **[CHECK whether you need it verbatim]**

### 20.3 Who is the reasonable person?
- **Not a real person.** It is a **fictional construct created by the common law** (a judicial construct).
- **Not the same as the average or ordinary person.**
  - Average people occasionally do unreasonable things, e.g. **checking their phone while driving**. Many people do this sometimes.
  - **A reasonable person would never check their phone while driving.**
  - So **what the average person does is not what the reasonable person does**.
- **Careful and prudent, and does the proper thing.** **Not perfect, but not irresponsible.**
- Something in between: think of **the average person always on their best behaviour**.
- Traditionally described as **"the man on the Clapham omnibus"**. **[CHECK: source. Usually attributed to *Hall v Brooklands Auto-Racing Club* [1933].]**
- The reasonable person is **"free both from over-apprehension and from over-confidence"**. Ordinary people have strengths and weaknesses; the reasonable person is **"just right"**. **[CHECK: source. Usually *Glasgow Corporation v Muir* [1943] AC 448, Lord Macmillan.]**

### 20.4 *Bolton v Stone* [1951] AC 850: no duty to eliminate every risk
- **Facts:** a cricket ball was **hit out of the ground** and injured P, who was standing on the road outside. P sued the **cricket club** in both **negligence** and **nuisance**, arguing the club breached a DOC owed to passers-by.
- **Held (House of Lords): no breach.**
  - A ball being hit out was **possible**, but the **frequency and likelihood were so tiny** that a reasonable person in the club's position would have been **justified in disregarding the risk**.
  - Being **ordinarily careful does not mean eliminating all potential risks**. Otherwise clubs would face a **huge burden** and **unrealistic measures** to prevent every unlikely risk.
  - "**An ordinary man does not take precautions against every foreseeable risk.**" He takes **certain precautions, but not all.**
- Link this to **optimal accident prevention** (§3.4).

## 21. How objective is the objective standard?

### 21.1 The general rule

| Direction | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Lowered?** | **Only for children**, in general. **Not** for **physical or mental incapacity**. |
| **Raised?** | **More often**, for people **professing or possessing special skills** (e.g. professionals). **[To be covered in later notes]** |

**Why no lowering for incapacity?**
- A reasonable person who is **blind, deaf or has trouble moving** will **take that into account in everything they do**. They are expected to **modify their behaviour to suit their own situation**.
- A **blind adult** can take account of being blind. An **8-year-old cannot take account of being 8**, which is why children are treated differently.
- E.g. a **98-year-old who can barely see** gets **no discount**. The required standard of care is **not lowered**.

> **Exam tip:** ask yourself **in what situations the standard would be lowered or raised**, and whether D falls into one of them.

### 21.2 Inexperience: *Nettleship v Weston* [1971] 2 QB 691
- **Issue:** should a **learner driver** be judged by a **lower standard of care**?
- **Held: no.** It is preferable to have a **reasonably certain standard of care**.
  - **Anyone operating a car** is expected to meet the standard of the **competent and experienced driver**.
  - There is **no such thing as a "brand new driver" standard**.
  - **A learner driver owes the same standard of care as any other driver.**
- *Link:* compulsory motor insurance (§6.3) is part of why courts are comfortable with this.

### 21.3 Children: the standard is lowered (objectively)
- **The one exception** to the general rule against lowering the standard.
- The standard is lowered **objectively**. It is **not what this particular child knew**, but what **a reasonable child of D's age and experience** would have foreseen.
- This is tricky and not very realistic, because **children of the same age develop very differently**.

**McHale v Watson (1966, High Court of Australia)** **[CASE TBC: confirm name]**
- A **12-year-old boy** threw a sharp object that glanced off a post and injured a girl.
- **Held:** judged by the standard of a **reasonable child of the same age, intelligence and experience**, **not an adult standard**, applied **objectively**.
  - The court does **not literally examine this boy's background**. It asks what a **typical boy of that age and type** would be expected to foresee and know.
- The court ruled **in favour of Watson** (D). **No breach**, because he **met the standard** of a reasonable 12-year-old.
- **Exception to the exception:** a child who **drives a car** is **held to the adult standard**. **[CHECK: authority]**

***Mullin v Richards* [1998] 1 WLR 1304** **[CHECK name]**
- **Facts:** two **15-year-old schoolgirls** were **play-fighting with plastic rulers**. One ruler **snapped**, and a fragment **partially blinded** one girl, who sued for the cost of the harm.
- **Held:** they were **close to adulthood but still children**, so the standard is that of a **reasonable 15-year-old**.
  - **Fencing with plastic rulers was common**, and this was a **freak accident**.
  - A **reasonable 15-year-old would not have foreseen the risk of serious injury**, so there was **no breach**.
